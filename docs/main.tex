\section{Introduction}

Wildlife individual re-identification (Re-ID) is the task of determining whether
different images show the same individual animal. This task is unlike species classification,
which asks what type of animal appears in an image, Re-ID asks which particular
individual is shown this distinction is important for wildlife monitoring
because the same individual may appear differently across photographs due to
changes in view point, lighting, pose, body condition, or environment, while
different individuals of the same species may have very similar appearances, we 
therefore require a reliable individual identification for monitoring without using physical tagging
or repeatedly handling animals~\cite{vidal2021perspectives}.

The traditional approaches for monitoring individual animals often rely on physical
tags, collars, or other forms of identification, which are effective in some
settings, these methods can be intrusive, expensive, and difficult to apply
consistently across many species and locations. Re-ID provides a
less intrusive alternative, where once an individual has been photographed, later
images can potentially be matched to that individual without additionally
capturing or handling. This can support conservation applications, such as
population monitoring, estimating animal abundance, and studying changes in
individuals over time~\cite{cermak2024wildfusion}.

However, reliable wildlife Re-ID remains difficult in unconstrained
photographic collections. Images may contain substantial variation in
background, view point, illumination, image quality, and animal pose. In
addition, individuals of the same species may have only subtle visual
differences. These challenges become more pronounced in large heterogeneous
benchmarks that combine images from different datasets, locations, and species, where
a representation that performs well on one collection may therefore fail to
generalise to another.

One possible way of addressing this problem is to use complementary visual
representations rather than relying on a single descriptor: (i) A global
representation can capture broad characteristics, such as body shape and
morphology, (ii) local features can capture fine-grained patterns, such as
spots, stripes, or other distinctive markings, and (iii) can be a general purpose visual
representation which may provide additional information that is not fully captured
by either global morphology or local correspondences. The combination of these
representations may therefore provide a more complete description of an
individual animal than any one of the representation alone. The recent work on calibrated
fusion has shown that combining global and local evidence can improve wildlife
Re-ID performance~\cite{cermak2024wildfusion}, while more recent approaches
have explored adding a third visual representation~\cite{kim2025fusion}.
This project investigates whether such complementary representations can
provide consistent benefits at the scale and diversity of WildlifeReID-10k~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}. 
The project focuses on calibrated fusion of three types of visual evidence, including a global wildlife
individual descriptor, a complementary visual representation, and local
key point based evidence. MiewID and a fine-tuned MegaDescriptor are considered
as alternative global representations, while EVA02 provides the complementary
representation and ALIKED provides local matching evidence their contributions
are evaluated individually and in combination to determine whether the
representations contain complementary information for individual level retrieval.

The project also considers whether the resulting fused representation remains
effective when the system encounters individuals that were not observed during
training. This is particularly important for wildlife monitoring because real
populations are not fixed: new individuals may enter a monitored population,
while previously observed individuals may disappear. The study therefore
evaluates both closed-set and open-set Re-ID using the evaluation protocols
provided by WildlifeReID-10k. In addition, because combining several
representations increases the dimensionality and storage requirements of the
resulting representation, the project investigates how much it can be
compressed before individual-level retrieval performance begins to degrade.
The main research objective is therefore to determine whether calibrated
fusion of global, complementary, and local visual representations provides a
more effective basis for wildlife individual Re-ID than individual
representations alone, and to establish how well this approach generalises
across the heterogeneous WildlifeReID-10k benchmark. The study further
investigates the behaviour of the fused representation under open-set
conditions and the extent to which it can be compressed while retaining
individual discriminative information.

The remainder of this proposal is organised as follows. Section~2 describes
the problem addressed by the project and defines the research questions.
Section~3 reviews the related work and identifies the research gap. Section~4
presents the proposed methodology and evaluation strategy. Section~5
justifies the proposed approach and summarises the expected contribution.

\section{Problem Description}

Wildlife individual re-identification becomes increasingly difficult as the
number and diversity of images and individuals increase. WildlifeReID-10k
combines 140,488 images of 10,772 individuals from 37 source datasets and approximately
33 species~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}. Unlike a small, single species
collection, this benchmark contains substantial variation in animal
appearance, image quality, photographic conditions, and the number of images
available for each individual. A method that performs well on one subset of
the data may therefore not necessarily generalise to the wider benchmark.

A major difficulty is learning individual specific information without
relying on incidental visual information. Wildlife images are often captured
in natural environments where backgrounds, lighting, vegetation, terrain, and
camera view points vary considerably, If the representation relies too heavily
on these factors, it may associate an individual with the environment in
which it was photographed rather than with characteristics of the animal
itself. Animal detection and cropping can reduce this risk by focusing the
subsequent representation on the animal, but the resulting representation
must still retain sufficiently detailed information to distinguish individuals
that may have very similar appearances.

This difficulty is closely related to the limitations of relying on a single
visual representation. Global descriptors can capture broad characteristics,
such as body shape and morphology, but may not fully represent small,
spatially localised differences between individuals. Local matching methods
can provide evidence from distinctive patterns, such as spots, stripes, or
scars, but their matching behaviour differs from that of fixed-length global
embeddings. A general purpose visual representation may provide additional
information that is not captured by either global morphology or local
correspondences. It is therefore necessary to determine whether these
different forms of visual evidence contain complementary information that can
be combined effectively, rather than assuming that a single representation is
sufficient for individual-level identification.

The problem is further complicated when the system encounters individuals
that were not observed during training. In a conventional closed-set setting,
the identities evaluated at test time are assumed to be represented in the
training data. The system can therefore select the most similar known
identity from a fixed set of candidates. In an open-set setting, this
assumption is relaxed: a query may belong to an individual that was not
observed during training. The system must consequently distinguish between a
sufficiently strong match to a known individual and a query for which no
known identity should be assigned. This setting is particularly relevant to
wildlife monitoring because populations are not static and new individuals
may appear over time.

These settings can be expressed formally. In the closed-set case, the test
identities are contained within the identities available during training,
\[
ID_{\mathrm{test}} \subseteq ID_{\mathrm{train}}.
\]
In the open-set case, at least some evaluated identities are absent from the
training identities,
\[
ID_{\mathrm{test}} \not\subseteq ID_{\mathrm{train}}.
\]
The open-set problem therefore requires not only ranking candidate identities
but also determining whether the similarity to the best candidate is
sufficiently strong to accept that identity.

WildlifeReID-10k also presents substantial variation in the number of images
available for different individuals. Some individuals are represented by many
images, while others have very few observations. A single pooled accuracy
value may therefore provide an incomplete picture of performance because
frequently observed individuals can contribute disproportionately to the
overall result. The benchmark's evaluation protocols and BAKS and BAUS
metrics provide a way of assessing performance while accounting for this
variation~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid,mendes2017open}. Using these benchmark
metrics also allows the proposed approach to be evaluated under the same
conditions as other methods developed for WildlifeReID-10k.

Taken together, these factors motivate the central problem addressed by this
project: \emph{whether multiple complementary visual representations can be
calibrated and fused to provide more reliable individual-level wildlife
Re-ID than any single representation alone, while remaining effective for
unseen identities and across the heterogeneous data represented in
WildlifeReID-10k.}

The project consequently investigates four related questions: (i) whether
MiewID or a fine-tuned MegaDescriptor provides the stronger global
representation on WildlifeReID-10k; (ii) whether combining the selected
global representation with EVA02 and ALIKED provides additional
individual-discriminative information compared with the individual
representations alone; (iii) whether the resulting fused representation
remains effective under the benchmark's open-set evaluation, where some
identities are not observed during training; and (iv) how much the fused
representation can be compressed before its individual-level retrieval
performance begins to degrade.

These questions define the scope of the research. The core investigation is
the representation and evaluation of calibrated multi-scale feature fusion.
The research pipeline therefore focuses only on the core research stages,
from visual representation and score calibration through fusion, compression,
retrieval, and evaluation.

\section{Related Work}

Wildlife individual re-identification has developed from
species-specific identification systems towards more general
multi-species representations. This development has been driven by the
increasing availability of wildlife imagery and by advances in
large-scale visual representation learning. WildlifeReID-10k reflects this
development by bringing together images from 37 source datasets and providing
both closed-set and open-set evaluation protocols designed to reduce
information leakage between training and testing data~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}.
The following work is therefore relevant to this project because it
establishes the progression from general-purpose visual representations to
wildlife-specific descriptors and, more recently, to calibrated fusion of
multiple complementary representations.

\emph{General-purpose visual representations.}
Self-supervised vision transformers provide a useful starting point for
learning visual representations without requiring task-specific labels.
DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging} introduced a self-distillation framework in
which different augmented views of an image are encouraged to produce
consistent representations. DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} extended this
approach through larger-scale and more carefully curated training data,
producing general-purpose visual features that transfer across many visual
tasks. In wildlife Re-ID, such general-purpose representations provide
useful baselines because they can encode visual information without having
been trained specifically to identify individual animals. However, their
general visual training does not directly optimise the fine-grained
individual-specific distinctions required by wildlife Re-ID. This motivates
the development and comparison of representations trained specifically for
animal identification.

\emph{Wildlife-specific global representations.}
WildlifeDatasets and the associated MegaDescriptor model represent an
important step towards general-purpose wildlife Re-ID. Cermak et
al.~\cite{cermak2024wildlifedatasets} introduced WildlifeDatasets as an
open-source framework for organising wildlife Re-ID datasets and training
models across multiple species. MegaDescriptor was developed as a
wildlife-specific foundation model for individual animal Re-ID, providing a
strong baseline against which more specialised approaches can be compared.
The WildlifeReID-10k benchmark also uses MegaDescriptor as a baseline,
reinforcing its relevance to large-scale multi-species evaluation. The
benchmark itself was introduced partly to address the limitations of
evaluating wildlife Re-ID on small, isolated datasets and to provide
similarity-aware splits that reduce leakage from visually similar images
appearing across training and test sets~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}.

MiewID provides another wildlife-specific global representation.
Otarashvili et al.~\cite{otarashvili2024multispecies} proposed MiewID as a
multi-species individual identification model based on an EfficientNetV2
backbone and an angular-margin training objective. Its multi-species
training and reported generalisation to species outside its training data
make it a relevant alternative to MegaDescriptor. Since MiewID and
MegaDescriptor provide different approaches to learning global
individual-level representations, there is no need to assume beforehand
that one is universally superior. This project therefore compares them on
the same WildlifeReID-10k evaluation setting before selecting the global
representation used in the proposed fusion.

\emph{Global and local feature fusion.}
Although wildlife-specific global descriptors provide strong individual
representations, they may not capture every fine-grained visual difference
between two individuals. Local feature matching provides a complementary
source of evidence by comparing spatially localised patterns between images.
WildFusion~\cite{cermak2024wildfusion} addresses this problem by combining
global similarity with local keypoint-based matching. Rather than directly
averaging raw scores, WildFusion calibrates the different similarity
measures before combining them, allowing global and local evidence to
contribute on a common scale.

This calibration step is particularly important because global embedding
similarities and local matching scores have different numerical
distributions. WildFusion therefore provides the methodological basis for
the fusion strategy investigated in this project. Its results demonstrate
that calibrated global-local fusion can improve wildlife Re-ID performance
over individual global representations, supporting the hypothesis that
different visual representations can contain complementary identity
information~\cite{cermak2024wildfusion}.

\emph{Multi-representation fusion.}
More recent work has extended global-local fusion by introducing a third
visual representation. Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025fusion} combined
MegaDescriptor, ALIKED, and EVA02 for the AnimalCLEF 2025 individual animal
Re-ID challenge. MegaDescriptor provided global visual features, ALIKED
provided local keypoint-based matching, and EVA02 supplied an additional
visual representation. The three components were combined using
WildFusion-style score calibration followed by weighted fusion. This work
provides direct motivation for the three-representation configuration
investigated in this project.

However, the role of the third representation should not be assumed to be
beneficial simply because it improves performance in one challenge setting.
The important research question is whether the additional representation
continues to provide complementary information when evaluated on a larger
and more heterogeneous benchmark. In particular, it remains necessary to
compare the fused system against its individual components under a common
evaluation protocol and determine whether the observed improvement is
consistent across the different species and source datasets represented in
WildlifeReID-10k.

\emph{Open-set wildlife re-identification.}
Open-set recognition is particularly important for wildlife Re-ID because
a deployed system cannot assume that every future observation belongs to an
individual previously registered in its database. WildlifeReID-10k
explicitly addresses this issue by providing open-set evaluation in addition
to closed-set evaluation. Its similarity-aware split was introduced to
reduce the effect of visually similar images appearing in both training and
testing data, which can otherwise lead to overly optimistic estimates of
Re-ID performance~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}.

The importance of open-set evaluation is also reflected in AnimalCLEF.
AnimalCLEF 2025 uses an open-set formulation in which the system must
determine both whether the query individual exists in the database and, when
it does, which identity it corresponds to. These
benchmarks demonstrate that wildlife Re-ID should not be evaluated only as
closed-set classification. A representation intended for practical
monitoring must also be able to reject sufficiently dissimilar queries
rather than assigning every query to a known identity.

\emph{Balanced evaluation across individuals.}
Wildlife datasets are often highly imbalanced because some individuals are
photographed much more frequently than others. This creates a problem for
standard pooled accuracy measures, which can allow frequently observed
individuals to dominate the evaluation. WildlifeReID-10k therefore provides
balanced evaluation measures for known and unknown identities, including
BAKS and BAUS, to provide a more informative assessment under its open-set
setting~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid}. These metrics are particularly relevant
to this project because the objective is not merely to improve performance
on frequently represented individuals, but to determine whether the fused
representation remains useful across the diverse identities represented in
the benchmark.

\emph{Representation compression and similarity retrieval.}
A multi-representation approach increases the amount of information that
must be stored and compared for each image. This introduces a secondary
practical question: how much of the resulting representation is necessary
to retain its individual-discriminative information? Principal Component
Analysis (PCA)~\cite{jolliffe2002pca} provides a standard approach for
reducing the dimensionality of high-dimensional representations while
retaining directions of greatest variance. However, the directions with
highest overall variance are not necessarily those that best distinguish
individual animals. Compression must therefore be evaluated empirically
rather than assumed to preserve individual identity information.

Similarity retrieval also becomes increasingly expensive as the gallery
grows. Approximate nearest-neighbour methods such as locality-sensitive
hashing (LSH)~\cite{indyk1998lsh} can reduce the number of comparisons
required during retrieval. In this project, however, retrieval efficiency
is treated as a supporting consideration rather than the primary research
objective. The main question is whether compression can reduce the
representation size while retaining individual-level retrieval performance.

\emph{Resulting research gap.}
The existing literature establishes several important foundations for this
project. General-purpose representations such as DINOv2 provide strong
visual baselines, while wildlife-specific models such as MegaDescriptor and
MiewID are designed specifically for individual animal identification.
WildFusion demonstrates that calibrated fusion of global and local evidence
can improve wildlife Re-ID, and the AnimalCLEF 2025 work demonstrates that a
third representation, EVA02, can be incorporated into a similar fusion
framework~\cite{cermak2024wildlifedatasets,cermak2024wildfusion,otarashvili2024multispecies,kim2025fusion}.

What remains less clear is whether this multi-representation approach
provides consistent individual-level benefits when evaluated at the full
scale and diversity of WildlifeReID-10k. In particular, it is not yet clear
whether the complementary information from global, local, and additional
visual representations remains useful across the benchmark's different
species and source datasets, whether the resulting representation remains
effective when identities are unseen during training, and how much of the
fused representation can be removed through dimensionality reduction before
individual-level discrimination begins to deteriorate. These questions
motivate the proposed evaluation of calibrated fusion using MiewID or
fine-tuned MegaDescriptor as the global representation, EVA02 as a
complementary visual representation, and ALIKED as the local representation
under WildlifeReID-10k's benchmark protocols.

\section{Proposed Approach}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} presents the proposed research methodology. The methodology focuses only
on the core research process used to investigate multi-scale feature fusion for wildlife individual
re-identification. WildlifeReID-10k images are first prepared through animal detection and cropping.
The cropped images are then processed using complementary visual representations: a global
wildlife-specific descriptor, a general visual descriptor, and a local keypoint-based descriptor.
Their similarity scores are calibrated before being combined through weighted fusion. The resulting
fused representation is subsequently evaluated for compression, similarity retrieval, and
closed-set and open-set re-identification. The methodology is designed to determine whether combining
different forms of visual evidence provides more reliable individual-level discrimination than using
a single representation.

\emph{Dataset and evaluation setting.}
The project uses WildlifeReID-10k~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid} as the primary evaluation benchmark.
The benchmark contains 140,488 images from 37 source datasets, covering approximately 33 species
and 10,772 individual animals. The experiments use the benchmark's published evaluation protocols
rather than creating an alternative splitting strategy, allowing the results to be compared with
existing work on the same benchmark.

The evaluation considers both closed-set and open-set settings. In the closed-set setting, the
identities represented in the evaluation data are known to the system, allowing the experiment to
measure how accurately the learned representations retrieve the correct individual. In the open-set
setting, some identities are not present during training, requiring the system to distinguish known
individuals from previously unseen ones. These two settings allow the project to evaluate both
individual-level retrieval and the ability of the proposed representation to generalise beyond
identities encountered during training.

\emph{Animal detection and cropping.}
Before extracting visual representations, each image is processed using MegaDetector
~\cite{beery2019megadetector}. The detector is used to localise the animal and produce a cropped
image containing the relevant subject. This step is intended to reduce the influence of background
information, such as vegetation, terrain, camera position, and lighting conditions, which may
otherwise provide information correlated with an individual's identity.

The same detection and cropping procedure is applied before extracting the representations used in
the experiments. This provides a more consistent input to the different descriptors and reduces the
possibility that differences in background content determine the observed performance differences
between representations.

\emph{Global representation.}
Two wildlife-specific global descriptors are considered. The first is MiewID
~\cite{otarashvili2024multispecies}, which is used as a pre-trained representation for individual animal
recognition. The second is MegaDescriptor~\cite{cermak2024wildlifedatasets}, which is fine-tuned on
the WildlifeReID-10k training data using an ArcFace-style angular-margin objective
~\cite{deng2019arcface}.

The purpose of considering both models is not to assume that one is universally superior, but to
determine which global representation provides the strongest basis for the subsequent fusion
experiments. Both representations are evaluated under the same retrieval protocol before the
stronger candidate is selected for the main fusion experiments.

The fine-tuning procedure is performed using training data only. Validation data are used for model
selection and parameter tuning, while the test data remain isolated until the final evaluation.
This separation prevents information from the evaluation set from influencing the learned global
representation.

\emph{Complementary representation.}
EVA02~\cite{fang2023eva02} is used as a second visual representation. Unlike MiewID and
MegaDescriptor, EVA02 is not specifically trained for wildlife individual re-identification.
Instead, it provides a general visual representation learned from large-scale image data. It is
therefore treated in this project as a complementary visual source rather than as a dedicated
wildlife identity descriptor.

The purpose of including EVA02 is to test whether information contained in a general visual
representation can provide additional discriminative information when combined with a
wildlife-specific global descriptor and local keypoint evidence. Its contribution is evaluated
through ablation experiments that compare the global representation alone with combinations of the
global, EVA02, and local representations.

\emph{Local representation.}
Local visual information is obtained using ALIKED~\cite{zhao2023aliked}. Unlike MiewID, MegaDescriptor,
and EVA02, ALIKED is not treated as a fixed-length image embedding. It detects local keypoints and
produces correspondences between two images. The resulting matching information provides evidence
about whether fine-grained spatial patterns are shared between the two images.

This local information is particularly relevant to individual re-identification because animals of
the same species may have similar overall morphology while differing in smaller visual details.
Depending on the species, these details may include spot arrangements, stripe patterns, markings,
scars, or other local structures. The local matching score is therefore treated as a complementary
source of identity evidence rather than being concatenated directly with the global embeddings.

\emph{General-purpose baseline.}
DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} is included as a general-purpose baseline. Its embeddings are extracted
without wildlife-specific fine-tuning and evaluated using the same retrieval framework as the
wildlife-specific representations.

The purpose of this baseline is to establish whether the proposed wildlife-specific and fused
representations provide an advantage over a strong general-purpose visual representation. DINOv2
does not form part of the proposed fusion itself; it is used as a separate comparison point.

\emph{Score calibration.}
The outputs of the different representations cannot be combined directly because they have different
numerical meanings and scales. Global descriptors produce vector similarities, typically measured
using cosine similarity, whereas ALIKED produces a keypoint-matching score. Directly averaging these
raw values could therefore cause one representation to dominate the fusion simply because its scores
have a different numerical range.

Following WildFusion~\cite{cermak2024wildfusion}, the raw similarity scores are calibrated before
fusion. Positive and negative image pairs are sampled from the training or validation data, and the
relationship between raw similarity scores and pairwise matching outcomes is learned using isotonic
regression. PCHIP interpolation is then used to obtain a smooth monotonic calibration function.

The calibration parameters are estimated without using the final test set. This ensures that the
test set remains unseen during calibration and provides an unbiased evaluation of the final system.

\emph{Multi-scale feature fusion.}
After calibration, the global, complementary, and local evidence is combined. The fusion is performed
at the similarity-score level rather than by directly concatenating the outputs of the three
models. This is necessary because ALIKED provides pairwise matching scores rather than a conventional
fixed-dimensional embedding.

Let $S_g$ denote the calibrated global similarity, $S_c$ the calibrated EVA02 similarity, and
$S_l$ the calibrated ALIKED similarity. The proposed fusion can be expressed as

\[
S_{\mathrm{fusion}}
=
w_g S_g
+
w_c S_c
+
w_l S_l,
\]

where

\[
w_g+w_c+w_l=1
\]

and each weight is non-negative.

The weights are selected using validation data and are not estimated using the final test set.
Different configurations are evaluated, including the global representation alone, global plus
local fusion, global plus complementary fusion, and the complete global-complementary-local fusion.
These comparisons provide an ablation-based test of whether each representation contributes useful
information beyond the others.

The global descriptor used in the final fusion is selected based on the comparison between MiewID and
fine-tuned MegaDescriptor. This prevents the choice of global descriptor from being assumed in advance
and allows the experiments to establish which representation provides the stronger foundation for
multi-scale fusion on WildlifeReID-10k.

\emph{PCA-based compression.}
The proposed fused representation is subsequently examined under different dimensionality
constraints. PCA~\cite{jolliffe2002pca} is applied to the fused representation using training data,
and several target dimensionalities are evaluated.

The purpose of this experiment is to determine how much dimensionality can be removed while
preserving individual-level discrimination. The unreduced representation is used as the reference
condition, and retrieval performance at progressively smaller dimensionalities is compared with this
reference.

Compression is therefore treated as an experimental variable rather than simply as an engineering
optimisation. The results will show whether the fused representation contains redundant information
and identify the point at which dimensionality reduction begins to negatively affect individual
re-identification performance.

\emph{Similarity retrieval.}
The resulting representation is used for similarity-based retrieval. Given a query image, the system
compares its representation with representations stored in the gallery and ranks candidate images
according to their similarity to the query.

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)~\cite{indyk1998lsh} may be used to accelerate similarity search for
the large gallery. However, the primary research outcome remains retrieval accuracy rather than
indexing performance. Retrieval latency and memory requirements are recorded as secondary measures
to characterise the practical cost of the proposed representation.

\emph{Closed-set evaluation.}
In the closed-set experiment, the correct individual is assumed to be represented within the known
identity set. For each query image, the gallery images are ranked according to the final similarity
score, and the position of the correct identity is used to evaluate retrieval performance.

The fused representation is compared against the individual descriptors and relevant ablation
configurations. This experiment directly addresses whether combining complementary visual evidence
improves individual-level retrieval compared with relying on a single representation.

\emph{Open-set evaluation.}
The open-set experiment evaluates whether the proposed representation can handle individuals that
were not observed during training. Rather than forcing every query to match an existing identity, the
system applies a decision threshold to determine whether the strongest gallery match is sufficiently
similar to be considered a known individual.

Let $S_{\max}$ denote the similarity score of the strongest gallery match. The decision is defined as

\[
\hat{y} =
\begin{cases}
\text{known identity}, & S_{\max} \geq \tau,\\
\text{unknown individual}, & S_{\max} < \tau,
\end{cases}
\]

where $\tau$ is the open-set decision threshold.

The threshold is selected using validation data and is not determined using the final test set.
Where supported by the benchmark protocol, thresholds are calibrated separately by species to
account for differences in visual similarity between individuals of different species. The final
evaluation is then performed on the held-out test data.

Performance is reported using the evaluation measures specified by WildlifeReID-10k, including the
closed-set and open-set metrics used by the benchmark. This allows the proposed method to be assessed
under the same evaluation framework as existing WildlifeReID-10k results.

\emph{Ablation and comparative evaluation.}
The experiments are structured to determine which parts of the proposed methodology contribute to
performance. The main comparisons include MiewID, fine-tuned MegaDescriptor, EVA02, ALIKED, and DINOv2
as individual or baseline representations, followed by combinations of global, complementary, and
local evidence.

The complete fusion is then compared with reduced fusion configurations. These comparisons allow the
project to determine whether improvements arise from the fusion itself or simply from using a
stronger individual descriptor. The compression experiments further determine whether the observed
improvements remain after reducing the dimensionality of the fused representation.

The final results are reported separately for closed-set and open-set evaluation and, where supported
by the benchmark, across the different species and source datasets. This makes it possible to assess
not only overall retrieval performance but also whether the observed benefit is consistent across
the heterogeneous data represented in WildlifeReID-10k.

The research methodology described above represents only the core experimental pipeline. Any
inference API, web application, or other software interface developed for demonstration or course
requirements is treated as a separate implementation deliverable. These components are not part of
the proposed research pipeline and are not used to define the scientific methodology or the
experimental evaluation.

\section{Justification and Conclusion}

The proposed study addresses the research gap identified in Section~3: although calibrated fusion
of global, local, and complementary visual representations has shown promising results for wildlife
Re-ID, it remains unclear whether these representations provide consistent additional information
when evaluated together at the scale and diversity of WildlifeReID-10k. Existing studies have
demonstrated the value of combining different sources of visual evidence, but the extent to which
this benefit transfers to a large benchmark containing many species, source datasets, and unseen
individuals remains insufficiently established. This project therefore evaluates the proposed
fusion directly on WildlifeReID-10k using its established evaluation protocols.

The choice of representations is motivated by the different types of information that they provide.
MiewID and MegaDescriptor provide wildlife-specific global representations that capture broader
visual characteristics associated with individual animals. EVA02 provides a general visual
representation that may contain information not captured by the wildlife-specific descriptor, while
ALIKED provides local correspondence information that can capture fine-grained spatial patterns.
Considering these representations together allows the project to test whether different forms of
visual evidence are complementary at the individual level. The direct comparison between MiewID and
fine-tuned MegaDescriptor also prevents the choice of global descriptor from being assumed in
advance.

Calibration is an important part of the proposed approach because the representations do not produce
directly comparable similarity scores. Applying calibration before fusion allows the contribution of
each representation to be combined on a common scale. The ablation experiments then provide a direct
way of determining whether the observed performance improvement is actually attributable to
complementary information from multiple representations rather than simply to the strength of one
individual descriptor.

The evaluation under both closed-set and open-set conditions addresses two different aspects of
individual re-identification. Closed-set evaluation measures the ability to retrieve the correct
individual when the relevant identity is represented in the known population. Open-set evaluation
extends this problem by requiring the system to recognise when a query does not correspond to a known
individual. This distinction is important for wildlife monitoring because populations are not static:
individuals may enter a monitored population or appear in the data without having been observed
previously. Evaluating both settings therefore provides a stronger assessment of whether the proposed
representation can support realistic individual-level re-identification.

The study also evaluates the proposed representation across the heterogeneous data contained in
WildlifeReID-10k. The benchmark combines multiple source datasets and species that differ in
appearance, image conditions, and the number of images available for each identity. Reporting
performance across these conditions allows the project to determine whether the benefit of fusion is
consistent or whether it depends strongly on particular species or source collections. This is
important because a method that performs well only on a subset of the benchmark would provide weaker
evidence for general multi-species wildlife re-identification.

Finally, the compression experiment investigates how much of the fused representation is actually
necessary for individual-level discrimination. PCA is used to produce representations of different
dimensionalities, and retrieval performance is compared with the unreduced representation. This
experiment does not assume that the highest-variance dimensions are necessarily the most useful for
identity recognition. Instead, it empirically determines whether dimensionality can be reduced while
maintaining useful individual-level information. Runtime and memory measurements are included as
secondary indicators of practical efficiency, while retrieval accuracy remains the primary research
outcome.

Taken together, these experiments provide a structured evaluation of multi-scale calibrated feature
fusion for wildlife individual Re-ID. The study does not assume that fusion will necessarily improve
performance. Instead, it tests whether global, complementary, and local representations provide
measurable additional information, whether that benefit extends to unseen individuals and diverse
species, and how much of the resulting representation can be removed before performance begins to
degrade. Comparisons with individual descriptors and DINOv2 provide appropriate reference points for
interpreting any observed improvement.

The expected contribution of the project is therefore empirical rather than a claim of a universally
superior re-identification system. The study aims to provide evidence about the usefulness,
generalisability, and compression tolerance of calibrated multi-scale feature fusion on a large,
heterogeneous wildlife Re-ID benchmark. If the fused representation consistently outperforms the
individual representations under the benchmark's evaluation protocols, this would support the
hypothesis that combining complementary visual evidence is beneficial for individual wildlife
re-identification. Conversely, limited or inconsistent improvements would provide evidence about the
conditions under which such fusion does not provide sufficient additional information to justify its
complexity.
